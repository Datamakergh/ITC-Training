From artificial intelligence and machine learning to deep learning, transformers and hallucinations

This is the longest section and the most important one for data labelers, because it explains exactly why labelled data matters. Learners will cover what machine learning is, how it differs from traditional programming, why we need it, how a machine learns, the role of data, and the four main types of learning. Transition line: “We said AI learns from examples. Machine learning is the part of AI that does that learning. Let’s look inside.”
|Generative AI
Key message: machine learning is learning from examples instead of following hand-written rules. Walk through the image left to right: data goes in, the machine is trained on it, the result of training is a “model”, and the model is what makes predictions. Define “model” clearly because learners will hear the word all day: a model is the thing that has learned the patterns. It is like a student’s knowledge after studying. Important caution in the third bullet: a model only works well on data similar to its training data. A model trained on photos of Ghanaian bank notes will not recognise Kenyan ones. This is why the variety of training data matters. Analogy: a child learning to recognise ripe mangoes. No one gives them a written rule. They see many mangoes, taste some, and learn that a certain colour and softness means ripe. Next time they see a mango they have never seen before, they can judge it.

Key message: this is the single most important shift to understand. In traditional programming, people give the computer the rules. In machine learning, people give the computer examples with answers, and the computer finds the rules. Notice that in the ML row the “Answers” are the labels. Ask learners: “In the spam example, who provides the answers?” (People who click “report spam”, or data labelers.) This is the moment to tell them: “Your job as a data labeler is to provide the answers that machines learn the rules from.” Why traditional programming struggles (from the original deck): - it needs exact instructions, and cannot handle complex tasks like understanding images or language well; - it cannot efficiently process huge amounts of data. Quick check: “To build an app that recognises 100 different plant diseases from photos, would you rather write the rules by hand or use ML?” Discuss why writing rules for every leaf colour and spot pattern is impossible.

Key message: we need machine learning because some problems are too complex for hand-written rules and some data is too big for people to handle. Reason 1, complex problems: ask learners to try writing rules to recognise a friend’s voice on the phone. It is impossible to describe in rules, yet a person learns it easily from experience. ML learns the same way. Reason 2, large volumes of data: a bank or mobile money operator handles millions of transactions a day. No human team could check each one for fraud in real time. An ML model can, flagging only the suspicious few for a person to look at. Link to their work: both examples depend on labelled data. A fraud model learns from past transactions labelled “fraud” or “genuine”.

Key message: beyond complex problems and big data, ML saves time, personalises experiences and keeps improving. Recommendation systems (an original module objective): when Netflix, YouTube, TikTok or Jumia recommend something, an ML model has learned from your past behaviour (what you watched, clicked, bought or skipped) and from people with similar tastes. It predicts what you are most likely to want next. Ask: “Has a recommendation ever been surprisingly accurate, or completely wrong? Key message: “learning” for a machine means guessing, checking the guess against the right answer, and adjusting, many thousands of times. Analogy that works well: a student preparing for an exam with past questions. 1. Data input = the past questions and the marking scheme. 2. Algorithm = the study method the student uses. 3. Training = practising the questions. 4. Feedback loop = checking answers against the marking scheme and seeing mistakes. 5. Iteration = practising again and again. 6. Gradient descent (mentioned in step 4) is simply the mathematical method for making small adjustments in the direction that reduces the error. Learners do not need the maths. Important: step 6 checks for “overfitting”, which is when a model memorises its training examples but fails on new ones, like a student who memorised last year’s answers but does not understand the subject.

Key message: building an ML system is a repeating cycle with three phases: data processing, modelling and deployment. Point out where the learners fit. Data processing (phase 1) usually takes most of the time on a real ML project, often quoted as around 80 percent. Data labeling is part of the “prepare / transform” step. Without it, there is nothing for the modelling phase to learn from. Why it is a cycle: once a model is in use, the world changes. New phone models, new slang, new kinds of fraud. Performance drops over time (this is called “model drift”), so teams collect fresh data, label it, and retrain. Ask: “Which step do you think is most often rushed, and what happens when it is?” Answer: data cleaning and labelling. A rushed dataset produces a model that looks fine in testing and fails in real life.

Key message: data is the foundation of machine learning, and the labels are the lesson. This is the slide that connects the module most directly to the learners’ work. Say it plainly: “The labels you create are the answer key the model learns from.” Ask: “What would happen if half the cat photos were accidentally labelled ‘dog’?” (The model gets confused and accuracy drops. It may even learn the mistake confidently.) Ask: “What if all our cat photos were of white cats indoors?” (It may fail on black cats or cats outside. This is how bias gets into AI.) The data split: - Training set (about 70%): the examples the model studies. - Validation set (about 15%): used during training to check progress and tune settings, like mock exams. - Test set (about 15%): kept completely hidden until the end, like the real exam. If the model has seen the test data, the score is meaningless. The 70/15/15 split is a common default, not a law. Learners will use this idea in the lab.

Key message: there are four main ways a machine can learn. The difference is what kind of data and feedback it gets. Key message: supervised learning is a loop of predict, compare, measure the error and update. Walk through the diagram from top to bottom. Use a concrete example, such as predicting the price of a used phone: 1. Input x: phone brand, age, storage, condition. 2. Prediction ŷ: the model guesses GH₵ 1,200. 3. Expected y: the real selling price from the label was GH₵ 1,500. 4. Error: the model was GH₵ 300 too low. 5. Update: it adjusts its internal settings so next time similar phones get a higher estimate. This repeats over thousands of phones until the predictions are close to the real prices. The symbols: x = input, y = true answer (label), ŷ (“y-hat”) = the model’s prediction. “Optimisation problem” just means finding the settings that make the error as small as possible.

Key message: reinforcement learning is learning from consequences, like training a pet with treats. Key characteristics (from the original deck): - Interaction-based learning: the agent learns by doing and getting feedback. - No labelled data: it learns by trial and error. - Common algorithms: Q-learning, SARSA, Deep Q-Networks (DQN). Key message: reinforcement learning is a loop of observe, act, get feedback, improve, repeated thousands or millions of times. Walk through the five steps with the self-driving car, then with something familiar: learning to play a mobile game. Key message: semi-supervised learning sits between supervised and unsupervised learning. It gets most of the benefit of labels while needing far fewer of them. Key message: deep learning is machine learning with many-layered neural networks, and its superpower is learning features straight from raw data. Explain “features”: features are the useful details a model looks at. To recognise a car in traditional ML, an engineer might hand-code features like “has four wheels” and “has windows”. In deep learning, the network discovers those features on its own from thousands of car photos. Walk through the image: in the top row (machine learning), a person extracts features, then the model classifies “car” or “not car”. In the bottom row (deep learning), the neural network does both steps itself. Trade-off to mention: deep learning needs much more data and computing power than traditional ML.
|Generative AI
Key message: a neural network turns a picture into numbers, passes them through layers of weighted connections, and the strongest output signal is its answer. Walk through slowly with a real photo of a dog on your phone if possible. Step 1: a small photo of 100 × 100 pixels already gives 10,000 numbers (30,000 for colour). The network never “sees” a dog; it sees numbers. Step 2: weights are importance values. At the start of training, the weights are random, so the network’s guesses are random too. Step 3: the output is a probability. “Dog 92%, cat 8%” means the network is fairly confident. Learning: when the network says “cat” but the label says “dog”, the error is sent backwards through the layers (this is called “backpropagation”) and each weight is nudged in the direction that would have given the right answer. After thousands of labelled photos, the weights are tuned well enough to recognise new dogs. Labelling link: the label “dog” on each training photo is what makes this correction possible.

Key message: this is one of the most important distinctions in the module. Deep learning models are either discriminative (they tell things apart) or generative (they create new things). Walk through the image: - Top row: a traditional ML model is given data and labels, learns the relationship between them, and outputs a label. - Bottom row: a generative AI model is given unstructured content (text, images), learns the patterns in that content, and outputs new content. Key message: neither type is “better”. They do different jobs, and the task decides which one to use. Walk through the customer service row, which comes from the original deck. Imagine a company’s help desk: - A discriminative model (for example logistic regression or an SVM trained on labelled past queries) reads the incoming message and sorts it into “Billing issue” or “Technical support”, so it reaches the right team. - A generative model (for example a GPT-style chatbot) writes a natural-sounding reply to the customer. Role
Who should the AI act as?
Task
What exactly should it do?
Context
Who is it for, and why?
Format
What shape should the answer take?
Limits
Length, tone, and what to do if unsure.
