Semantic segmentation labels every pixel. Point out that each colour is a class, not an individual object: all cars share one colour. Emphasise that no pixel may be left unlabelled.

Polylines are for things that are long and thin, such as lane markings, kerbs, cables and rivers. The annotator clicks points along the line; more points are needed on curves.
Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5
Polygons give a much tighter fit than a box, which matters for irregular shapes like people and trees. Compare the tree outline here with the bounding box on the next slide.
Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5
Bounding boxes are the fastest and most common technique. The key quality rule is tightness: the box touches the object’s edges with no gap and nothing cut off. Even small, distant cars get their own box.
Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5
Key points are about precise position and consistent order: left shoulder is always point 6, for example. When a joint is hidden, annotators still mark it but flag it as occluded, as the guideline specifies.
Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5
Two areas with very different character, which is why they are presented differently. LiDAR is annotation in three dimensions. It needs suitable data, suitable tools and more practice than the 2D techniques, navigating a point cloud is the hardest part for new annotators, not the Labeling rule. Big Data is not annotation at all. It is the surrounding engineering: where the data comes from, how it is cleaned and stored, and how production and quality figures are reported back. Annotators rarely see it, but every delay in data preparation shows up later as idle annotation capacity. If asked how these relate to the training: LiDAR techniques appear in the annotation week as 3D bounding box and 3D semantic segmentation; Big Data does not, and is a separate service line.
Day 1Day 2Day 3Day 4Day 5
A LiDAR scan is a cloud of 3D points measured by laser. 3D segmentation gives every point a class, the 3D version of semantic segmentation. Point out the rings on the ground (each is one laser beam) and the dark gaps behind cars and people, where the laser cannot see.

Unlike a 2D box, a cuboid has depth and a heading. The white line on top of each box shows which way the object faces; the oncoming car’s box points the other way. Boxes should hug the points and keep the same size for the same object across frames.

After the five-day data labeling training, graduates can start as labelers and grow up the ladder to Quality Manager, Project Manager and Head Project Manager, with further training such as the ToT. They can also move across into related roles that use the same skills: labeling and annotation roles (including 3D and LiDAR work after the point-cloud introduction), quality and review roles, digital office support roles that use the Office basics from Day 1, and AI and digital creation roles that build on Generative AI, prompting and the vibe-coding project. These are possible pathways, not guaranteed placements.

On the left is the routine labelers practise every day of the training: read the guideline, label in SYNAPSE, check and fix work after inspection, then report daily output, issues and reflection. On the right, SYNAPSE sits at the centre because once you know it, other tools feel familiar. Image editors and design tools use the same zoom, layers, selection and shape logic as the labeling canvas. ArcGIS works with polygons on map layers, like polygon labeling. Excel, PowerPoint and Word are taught on Day 1 and are used to track and report output. Knowing how labeled data trains AI helps you use Generative AI well. Other labeling platforms such as CVAT or Label Studio run the same tasks with a different interface. Photoshop, GIMP, Figma, Canva, ArcGIS, CVAT and Label Studio are suggested next steps, not part of the training.

QUESTION 1: When the car is upside down in the air, can a person react fast enough? (No. Things happen in fractions of a second, so the car's computer must control the thrusters.) QUESTION 2: Automated or autonomous? (Good discussion! The stunt is planned and practised in a closed area: the computer follows a plan and keeps the car balanced, which is mostly AUTOMATED. AUTONOMOUS would mean deciding by itself in new, busy places, like a city. The engineer says it cannot do that.) QUESTION 3: Is it ready for the road? (No. Maybe production in 2028, and it must pass safety rules. Exciting videos are not the same as a finished product: always check the facts, as we learned in Part 4.)
